# Scroll typography pacing for a one-page portrait

Scope note for the report writer: this is for a one-page personal portrait reached from an X profile, where the sentences are the piece and motion is how they are delivered. It is not a product landing page. No implementation is included. Figures below are only those printed on the cited pages. Where a page is old, the date from the page is given. Searches did not turn up a studio postmortem that measured how long a pinned type sequence can run before people leave.

## What do studies or practitioner teardowns say about scroll-hijacking, pin length, and when people abandon?

### Takeaway
Nielsen Norman Group does not publish a pin-length limit or a scrolljacking abandonment rate. What it does publish is that people decide to leave in the first 10–30 seconds, that most viewing time stays in the first two or three screenfuls, and that motion which moves or replaces text on its own makes that text harder to read and easier to ignore. A dedicated NN/g “scrolljacking” article was not found, and no studio postmortem with a measured pin threshold was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g’s summary of dwell time: “Users often leave Web pages in 10–20 seconds, but pages with a clear value proposition can hold people's attention for much longer. To gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds.” The same article says the average page visit lasts a little less than a minute, and that the average is a poor summary because visits vary so widely. — [How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/) (published 2011-09-12)
- That article is NN/g’s reading of Chao Liu, Ryen W. White, and Susan Dumais, “Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time,” SIGIR 2010. NN/g reports the dataset as 205,873 pages with upwards of 10,000 visits each, more than 2 billion dwell times, and says 99% of pages showed “negative aging”: the longer someone has already stayed, the less likely they are to leave on the next instant. NN/g’s description of the fitted hazard curve: the first 10 seconds are when leaving is very likely; people are still highly likely to leave during the next 20 seconds; after about 30 seconds the curve flattens; staying half a minute means a fair chance of staying much longer, “often 2 minutes or more.” These second-by-second readings are NN/g’s account of the chart, not numbers re-checked against the SIGIR paper in this pass. — [How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/); underlying paper linked there as [Liu, White, and Dumais, SIGIR 2010](https://dl.acm.org/citation.cfm?doid=1835449.1835513)
- 2018 NN/g eyetracking: more than 130,000 fixations from 120 participants on a 1920×1080 screen, across news, ecommerce, blogs, FAQs, and encyclopedic pages, compared with a 2010 study on 1024×768 screens. Users spent about 57% of page-viewing time above the fold. 74% was spent in the first two screenfuls (up to 2160px). The second screenful got about 17%. The remaining 26% was a long tail. NN/g explicitly says this can be caused by short pages or by people giving up after two screenfuls. On general sites, more than 42% of viewing time fell in the top 20% of the page and more than 65% in the top 40%. In 2010, 80% of viewing time was above the fold; NN/g says that by this study the fold, as a barrier, had moved to about the third screenful, with 81% of viewing time in the first three screenfuls. They also say people rarely go beyond the third screenful. — [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) (published 2018-04-15)
- The same article says the interaction cost of scrolling lowers the chance that lower content is seen, that attention still clusters at the top, and that very long pages raise the risk of losing attention. It warns against a “false floor”: minimal layouts can look finished and stop scrolling unless something is cut off or otherwise signals that there is more. — [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- Illusion of completeness (term attributed to Bruce Tognazzini, 1998): the screen looks finished when more content is off-screen. NN/g lists full-viewport heroes, full-width horizontal rules, and large empty gutters as patterns that create a false floor. In one test of Maple.com, whose hero video filled the screen, six of eight users did not realize they could scroll; NN/g says 75% of those test users were frustrated. That is a single eight-person task, not a population rate. — [The Illusion of Completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/) (published 2016-01-17)
- Horizontal scrolling on desktop “is one of the few interactions that consistently generate negative responses from users.” NN/g’s reasons: the sideways scrollbar is hard to steer, people do not expect content off to the side, and even arrows are weak information scent. One USA Today participant, asked about side arrows: “I wouldn’t use these. I want to read what I want to read.” The article is about sideways movement and “swiping,” not about vertical pin length. Artist and agency sites are named as the places that scroll sideways in order to look distinctive; NN/g says being different for its own sake is a bad rationale on mainstream sites. — [Beware Horizontal Scrolling and Mimicking Swipe on Desktop](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-scrolling/)
- Auto-forwarding: in a UK study, a Siemens homepage failed a task whose answer was in a large rotating panel. The panel advanced every 5 seconds. The participant: “I didn't have time to read it. It keeps flashing too quickly.” She then scrolled and never saw the full box again. NN/g’s rule from that failure: accordions and carousels should change only when the user asks, otherwise stand still. Listed harms include less time for low-literacy and non-native readers, motor difficulty hitting a moving target, lower chance of seeing any one item (in that example the deal was visible 20% of the time), loss of control, and motion making the block look like an ad. — [Auto-Forwarding Carousels and Accordions Annoy Users and Reduce Visibility](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/) (published 2013-01-20)
- Carousels: “on large or small viewports, people often scroll past carousels.” People miss every frame except, at best, the first. Designers who know later frames exist can get “a false sense of security” that the set will be understood from one frame. A static hero, or content placed in the page, is offered as the alternative. This page, as retrieved, does not state a click-through percentage. — [Carousel Usability: Designing an Effective UI for Websites with Content Overload](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- Reading volume, from Harald Weinreich and colleagues’ 2005 naturalistic logs (25 instrumented browsers; NN/g later used 45,237 page views after dropping visits under 4 seconds, visits over 10 minutes, and pages under 20 words): for pages of 30 to 1,250 words, people spent about 25 seconds plus 4.4 seconds per additional 100 words. At an assumed 250 words per minute, that is 18 words per 100 added words, or 18% of added copy. On a 593-word average page, NN/g says users have time to read at most 28% of the words if every second is spent reading, and that about 20% is the more realistic figure. — [How Little Do Users Read?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) (published 2008-05-06); study: Weinreich, Obendorf, Herder, and Mayer, ACM Transactions on the Web, 2008, linked from that article
- An older NN/g reading study, restated on the current “How Users Read on the Web” page: 79 percent of test users scanned a new page; 16 percent read word by word. Concise copy measured 58% more usable than promotional “marketese,” and scannable copy 47% more usable, in a five-version task study described on that page. — [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)
- Response-time limits NN/g still cites (Miller 1968; Card, Robertson, and Mackinlay 1991; Nielsen’s 1993 *Usability Engineering*, with a 2014 restatement): about 0.1 second to feel instantaneous / directly manipulating; about 1 second before the flow of thought breaks; about 10 seconds as the limit for keeping attention on the dialogue. The 2014 note says anything slower than 10 seconds needs a percent-done indicator and “a clearly signposted way for the user to interrupt,” and that after more than 10 seconds people must reorient. These limits are about waiting on the system, not about how many pixels a pin should occupy. The same essay says the computer can also be too fast for a person to stop a scrolling list on the item they wanted, so motion should be timed to a clock, not to raw machine speed. — [Response Times: The 3 Important Limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/); [Powers of 10: Time Scales in User Experience](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/powers-of-10-time-scales-in-ux/) (published 2009-10-05)
- Animation that is meant to show cause and effect “must begin within 0.1 seconds of the initial user action,” or it no longer feels like direct manipulation. Repeated animation with no purpose beyond novelty becomes a roadblock; NN/g reports hearing, in tests, variants of “this was nice the first time, but now it’s getting annoying.” The Newton Running menu is cited because the transition could not be skipped and played again on every navigation. Peripheral motion (slides, pops) pulls attention off the reading task; a slow fade with no position change pulls less. — [Animation for Attention and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/) (published 2014-09-21)
- GSAP documents `ScrollTrigger.normalizeScroll(true)` as forcing scroll onto the JavaScript thread so updates stay in sync and, on most phones, the address bar stops showing and hiding. GSAP calls the address-bar resize problem real, says ScrollTrigger must then recalculate start and end positions, and says the resulting jump “isn’t a bug in ScrollTrigger.” It also says this API “is a hybrid form of scroll-jacking”: it cancels native scroll events and reapplies the delta in JavaScript, but it does not invent a fake scrollbar or a different acceleration. It prevents overscroll bounce. Known exception stated by GSAP: recent iOS, phones, portrait, where the browser still forces the address bar to show and hide. — [ScrollTrigger.normalizeScroll()](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/static.normalizeScroll())
- The ScrollTrigger overview shows pinning and scrubbing as ordinary tools, including an example with `pin: true`, `end: "+=500"` (500px of extra scroll), and `scrub: 1` (“takes 1 second to ‘catch up’ to the scrollbar”). That is sample configuration, not a stated maximum length or a usability finding. — [ScrollTrigger](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/)

### Inferences
- For a portrait reached from X, the harsh window is the opening seconds, not a long exploratory session. The sentence that matters has to be readable inside that window. A pin that withholds the only readable line until the visitor has already spent the first screenfuls scrolling is using the part of the page where NN/g measured most of the viewing time (57% above the fold, 74% in two screenfuls) on motion instead of on the text.
- “Too much” is not a published pin length. The closest published edges are: the value has to be clear within about 10 seconds or the leave-hazard stays high through about 30 seconds; attention is thin after about three screenfuls; an operation with no interrupt past about 10 seconds is where NN/g says attention leaves a dialogue. Those are different studies. Mapping them onto one pinned type sequence is an inference, not a measured result.
- Scroll-hijacking, in the sources actually retrieved, shows up as two different complaints. NN/g’s evidence is about losing the ability to read because something moves, replaces itself, or hides on an axis people do not expect. GSAP’s own docs use “scroll-jacking” for a narrower technical move: taking the scroll delta off the browser thread so a pinned scene does not jump when the mobile chrome resizes. Those are not the same offense. Cancelling native scroll for a long distance, so the scrollbar no longer tracks readable text, is the version that collides with the NN/g findings. Normalizing a few pixels of chrome jitter is the version GSAP defends.
- A full-viewport line with empty space around it can act as the false floor NN/g describes. If the first frame looks like a finished poster, the six-of-eight Maple result is the relevant caution: people do not scroll when nothing tells them the page continues. Cut-off type, or a clearly partial line, is the kind of signifier that article says works. A hairline rule is the kind of signifier it says can do the opposite.

### Gaps
- `https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking/` and `https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-hijacking/` both returned 404 on 8 October 2026. No NN/g page using the word scrolljacking was retrieved. Horizontal scrolling, auto-forwarding, illusion of completeness, and scrolling-and-attention are the nearest NN/g pieces found.
- No studio postmortem (Active Theory, Resn, or similar) and no teardown with a measured abandon rate for a pinned typography sequence were retrieved. Bing’s RSS results for those queries were unrelated. `https://www.niemanlab.org/?s=archie+tse` returned 403. `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrolljacking` returned 404. `https://alistapart.com/article/designing-safer-web-animations-for-motion-sensitivity/` returned 404. A Viljami Salminen scrolljacking URL failed DNS. The Archie Tse / New York Times “readers will scroll but will not click” talk was not found, so it is not used.
- The Liu, White, and Dumais paper was not read. Do not treat NN/g’s 10-, 20-, 30-second, and 2-minute descriptions as quotations from the paper.
- No source found states a number of viewport-heights or seconds at which a pin becomes a trap.

## How long should a pinned type sequence be before it feels like a trap? What do strong sites do for a skip, a nav jump, or an end state that is readable if you never scroll?

### Takeaway
Nothing retrieved sets a maximum pin length. The sources do say that an unskippable animation is already a failure when it blocks reading, that only the resting frame can be counted on, and that a screen which looks complete will not be scrolled. The end state if the visitor never scrolls therefore has to be a readable sentence, not a mid-gesture frame.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g on Newton Running: the menu animation had “no way to skip” once triggered, and NN/g treats that repetition as annoying enough that a third viewing may never happen because the person leaves. The complaint is unskippable motion standing between the person and the content, not the existence of motion. — [Animation for Attention and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/)
- WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (Level A): for moving, blinking, or scrolling information that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds, and is shown in parallel with other content, there must be a mechanism to pause, stop, or hide it, unless the movement is essential. Separately, auto-updating information that starts automatically and runs in parallel with other content needs a way to pause, stop, or hide it, or to control the update frequency, and the understanding document says there is no five-second exception for auto-updating. “Starts automatically” means it was not the direct result of an intentional activation such as choosing a link or button. Auto-updating includes content that “updates or disappears based on a preset time interval,” and the examples include “auto-advancing presentations.” Pausing so the person can read, then resuming from that point, is described as the better behavior when the content is not a live status. — [Understanding 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- WCAG 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (Level AAA): motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled unless it is essential. The understanding document’s distinction: 2.2.2 is for animation the page starts by itself; 2.3.3 is for animation the person’s action starts. Scrolling new content into view is called essential and allowed, because the person controls it. Extra motion attached to that scroll (the example is decorative elements moving horizontally while the page scrolls vertically, and parallax) is non-essential and should be avoidable, either by not using it, by a control, or by the operating system’s reduced-motion setting. A quoted persona line in that document: “Stop that extra movement! You are making me so dizzy I cannot concentrate.” — [Understanding 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- “Essential” in that document: removing it would fundamentally change the information or functionality, and the information cannot be achieved another way that would conform. — [Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- Carousel guidance again: do not count on any frame except the one actually on screen; people scroll past the block; a single static hero is the safer default when the message has to land. — [Carousel Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/)
- False floor: if the visible screen looks complete, people do not go looking for the rest. Large heroes with no continuation cue are the example. — [The Illusion of Completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/); [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- GSAP’s documented sample pin ends after an additional 500px of scroll (`end: "+=500"`). That shows a short pin is a normal use of the tool. It is not a researched maximum, and the same page does not discuss a skip control. — [ScrollTrigger](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/)

### Inferences
- A pinned type sequence starts to feel like a trap when the visitor is scrolling and the readable sentence is not changing in step with that effort: either the same frame holds while scroll distance is consumed, or the text moves but never settles into something that can be read. The NN/g carousel failure (“I didn't have time to read it”) and the unskippable Newton Running transition are the two documented versions of that feeling. Neither one supplies a pixel count.
- For three or four lines, the non-trap shape implied by these sources is: one line already readable with no scroll (that is the frame people will actually see, and it has to carry the value proposition inside the first 10 seconds); a short stretch of scroll that brings the other lines in and then lets go, on the order of a fraction of a screen rather than several screens, because most viewing time is already gone after two screenfuls; and a resting layout, after the pin releases or if the visitor never moves, in which every sentence is on the page in order. A skip control matters when the sequence starts by itself or cannot be cut short; a sequence the visitor drives by scrolling already has a stop, which is to stop scrolling, provided the text they have reached is readable.
- A nav jump does not replace that resting text. These sources never describe a portrait site’s anchor nav as the fix for a pin. They describe the failure as “the thing on screen cannot be read, and there is no way to make it hold still.” If the only copy of a line lives past the pin, a nav link that depends on scrolling into that pin does not help the person who will not scroll.
- “Strong sites” here could not be checked against a teardown. The practitioner pattern that is actually written down is Apple-adjacent only in the sense that GSAP’s own docs teach scrubbing a pinned scene to the scrollbar so the visitor, not a timer, advances it. That is a tool pattern, not evidence that long product-page pins are appropriate for a personal page. Product pages can spend many screens on one object because the object is the point. A portrait whose point is four sentences cannot spend the high-attention region hiding three of them.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source describes what specific contemporary personal sites do for a “skip,” a section jump, or a no-scroll end state. WCAG 2.4.1 Bypass Blocks was not fetched and should not be stretched into a “skip this pin” requirement; 2.2.2’s pause/stop/hide is the mechanism these notes can actually cite, and it applies to automatic motion, not to user-scrubbed scroll.
- No source says “a pin of N viewport-heights is acceptable and N+1 is a trap.”

## How should a sequence of three or four lines be paced: time-based on load versus scrubbed to scroll?

### Takeaway
For a short personal piece, the sources point to a readable line on load and scroll as the clock for the rest. A timer that swaps lines is the auto-advancing pattern NN/g and WCAG 2.2.2 treat as a reading failure. Scrubbing keeps the pace with the visitor, which WCAG treats as the allowed part of scroll, but only while the scrub is actually tracking the scrollbar and the text can be read at rest.

### Cited Findings
- A preset interval that replaces what is on screen is the behavior NN/g says failed at Siemens: the panel changed every 5 seconds, the participant could not finish reading, and the advice is to advance only on request and otherwise stand still. Motion itself also made the block easier to dismiss as an advertisement. — [Auto-Forwarding Carousels and Accordions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/auto-forwarding/)
- WCAG treats “auto-advancing presentations” and anything that “updates or disappears based on a preset time interval” as auto-updating. Auto-updating that starts by itself and sits beside other content needs pause, stop, hide, or rate control, with no five-second exemption. Moving or scrolling that starts by itself has the five-second clause: a mechanism is required if it lasts more than five seconds and is parallel with other content, unless essential. An animation can be essential during a preload where nobody can interact yet and silence would look broken. A title sequence shown next to a nav, or instead of the sentence, is not that case. — [Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- User-driven scroll is the other category. WCAG 2.3.3 says bringing new content into view by scrolling is essential and allowed; extra motion on top of that scroll is what has to be optional. — [Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- Direct manipulation, per NN/g, needs the effect to start within about 0.1 second of the action. Between 0.1 and 1 second the person still holds the thought but feels the computer is doing it. Past about 1 second, flow breaks. Past about 10 seconds, attention leaves unless there is progress feedback and a way to interrupt. — [Animation for Attention and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/); [Response Times: The 3 Important Limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- GSAP’s own sample of a smooth scrub is `scrub: 1`, glossed as taking one second to catch up to the scrollbar. An immediate scrub is the other option the overview describes (`scrub: true` is the scrubber that “acts like a scrubber”). The one-second catch-up is a smoothness knob in the docs, not a recommended reading pace. — [ScrollTrigger](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/)
- NN/g’s animation article separates motion that explains a change the person just caused from motion that exists to look current or delightful. The questions it says to answer first: where attention would otherwise be, whether the goal is attention, continuity, or a relationship between things already in view, how often this person will see it, and whether the person caused it or it started on load or on scroll. Indirect motion (on load, or while scrolling, from some other activity) is the case it says to justify, not the default. — [Animation for Attention and Comprehension](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-usability/)
- People do not read much of a page. About 20% of the words on an average visit is NN/g’s realistic figure, and 79% of users in the older study scanned rather than read. A four-line sequence can be the whole text a visitor will take. A timed crossfade that never holds a line still spends that budget on transitions. — [How Little Do Users Read?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/); [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)
- web.dev’s reduced-motion article separates three uses of motion: feedback that an action was received, perception hacks that make waiting feel shorter, and decorative motion (animated gradients, parallax, background video). Decorative motion is the category some people like and others need removed. A type sequence whose motion *is* the sentence is closer to the first use only while it is tied to the visitor’s act of scrolling. Played on a timer at load, it is the page moving by itself. — [prefers-reduced-motion: Sometimes less movement is more](https://web.dev/articles/prefers-reduced-motion)

### Inferences
- The tradeoff the retrieved sources actually support, for a piece this short: show one complete line immediately, so the 10-second judgment has something to judge, and scrub the gathering of the next lines to scroll, so the visitor can stop on a line and read it. That uses motion as the communication (the lines assemble because the person moved) without the auto-advance failure (the line disappears because a clock fired).
- A pure title card on load is the worse default for three or four lines if each line replaces the last. That is an auto-advancing presentation. Even a single pass under five seconds still has to leave a readable end state, because 2.2.2’s auto-updating clause does not grant a five-second pass for content that appears and then is gone, and because NN/g’s reader never got back to a panel that had already rotated away.
- A pure scrub with nothing readable at scroll position zero fails the other test. The person who does not scroll, or who looks only at the first screen, gets no sentence. Given 57% of viewing time above the fold and the false-floor finding, the opening frame cannot be an empty or partial hold waiting for a gesture.
- `scrub: 1` (one second of catch-up) fights the 0.1-second direct-manipulation limit. For type, where the visitor is trying to park a line in the center of their vision and read it, lag between the finger and the words is the “computer is doing it” feeling NN/g describes past 0.1 second, and at a full second it is the edge of broken flow. Smoothing is a decorative delay on top of an interaction the person is already controlling. The sources do not say “never smooth.” They do say the cause-and-effect animation has to begin effectively immediately, and that extra motion on scroll is the part to remove for anyone who does not want it.
- Pacing within the scrub: NN/g’s reading data says people scan and take about a fifth of the words. Each line should be able to sit still under the eye. A scrub that only passes through a line at a readable size for a few pixels will reproduce the Siemens problem with the scrollbar as the timer. The hold is the design. The move between holds is the motion.

### Gaps
- No essay from GSAP, Codrops, or a studio was found that says, in so many words, “for a short personal site, choose scrub over a load timeline” or the reverse. The tradeoff above is an inference from NN/g, WCAG, web.dev, and the GSAP API notes, not a quotation from a studio retrospective.
- No source gives a recommended duration per line for a four-line sequence.

## Mobile: what breaks when large type is position:absolute inside overflow:hidden and scrubbed with vw/vh offsets?

### Takeaway
The stage and the type are sized from different boxes. `vh` and `vw` follow the large viewport, which assumes the browser chrome is retracted, while `position: absolute` is positioned against a positioned ancestor or the initial containing block, and `overflow: hidden` clips at the padding box. On a phone those three facts combine: offsets jump or sit under the URL bar, wide `vw` shifts create a horizontal scrollbar that authors then hide, and anything scrubbed outside the stage is clipped with no scrollbar to bring it back. GSAP separately documents that the address bar showing and hiding changes trigger math and makes pinned scrubbing jump, including on current iOS portrait where that show/hide cannot be suppressed.

### Cited Findings
- CSS Values Level 4: `vw` is 1% of the viewport’s width and `vh` is 1% of the viewport’s height. The default viewport-percentage units (`v*`, which includes `vh`/`vw`) and the large units (`lv*`) are both defined against the large viewport: the viewport sized as if dynamically expanded and retracted browser interfaces are retracted. — [CSS Values and Units Module Level 4, viewport-percentage lengths](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-values-4/#viewport-relative-lengths)
- Chrome’s “URL bar resizing” note states the practical result: `vh` does not resize as the URL bar shows or hides; it is the large viewport, so `100vh` is taller than the visible area while the URL bar is shown. The initial containing block does the opposite: it stays the small viewport, as if the URL bar were always showing, and does not grow when the bar hides. `position: fixed` elements whose containing block is the initial containing block are the exception and do resize with the URL bar. `position: absolute` is not given that exception. Chrome says this split was chosen to match Safari on iOS. A demo on that page compares `99%`, `99vh`, `position: fixed`, and `position: absolute` on one scrollable page. — [URL bar resizing](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/url-bar-resizing)
- CSS-Tricks, citing the same class of bug: an element of `100vw` is wider than the layout once a vertical scrollbar exists, which creates a horizontal scrollbar, and authors then hide overflow on the body to compensate. On mobile, `100vh` does not shrink when browser chrome (navigation, keyboard) appears, so chrome covers content that was placed at the bottom of `100vh`. The article points at `-webkit-fill-available` as a partial workaround and at the later large/small/dynamic viewport units as the intended model. — [CSS fix for 100vh in mobile WebKit](https://css-tricks.com/css-fix-for-100vh-in-mobile-webkit/); [The Large, Small, and Dynamic Viewports](https://css-tricks.com/the-large-small-and-dynamic-viewports/), which quotes Bram Van Damme: the dynamic viewport (`dvh`) sits between `100vh` (maximum, chrome retracted) and `100svh` (minimum, chrome expanded) and changes as the UI shows or hides. — also [Bram.us](https://www.bram.us/2021/07/08/the-large-small-and-dynamic-viewports/)
- MDN, `position: absolute`: the element is taken out of flow and positioned against its closest positioned ancestor, or else the initial containing block; `top` / `right` / `bottom` / `left` set the final position. `position: fixed` is positioned against the initial containing block, “which is the viewport in the case of visual media,” not against a nearby stage. — [position](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/position)
- MDN, `overflow: hidden`: “Overflow content is clipped at the element's padding box, hiding the clipped content.” The box is a scroll container with no scrollbars; programmatic scrolling and tabbing to a focusable clipped element can still move it, but there is no bar for a finger to drag. — [overflow](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/overflow)
- WebKit’s iPhone X article is about a related inset, not about type scrubbing: content laid out to the raw screen edge is covered by the sensor housing and the home indicator unless padding accounts for `safe-area-inset-*` via `env()`, after `viewport-fit=cover`. Large type offset toward the edges with viewport units can land in that covered region. — [Designing Websites for iPhone X](https://webkit.org/blog/7929/designing-websites-for-iphone-x/)
- GSAP on the scrub itself, on phones: when the address bar shows or hides, the viewport resize changes ScrollTrigger’s start and end, and the scene jumps. `normalizeScroll(true)` is their mitigation and, as noted above, does not work around current iOS portrait, where the browser forces the chrome to move. The same mode turns off rubber-band overscroll. GSAP also notes that native scrolling often runs on a separate thread from JavaScript, so repainting a scrub can be slightly out of sync until scroll is forced onto the JavaScript thread. — [ScrollTrigger.normalizeScroll()](https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Plugins/ScrollTrigger/static.normalizeScroll())
- MDN’s scroll-event page warns that `scroll` handlers fire often enough to cause jank if they do expensive DOM work, and that wrapping the handler in `requestAnimationFrame` does not throttle them because animation frames are already fired at that rate. That is the desktop-side cost of hand-rolled scrubbing. It does not say that mobile only emits the event when the gesture ends; that claim was not found and is not used. — [Document: scroll event](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Document/scroll_event)

### Inferences
- A stage with `overflow: hidden` and a child `position: absolute` whose `top`/`left` are in `vh`/`vw` will not stay inside the stage across URL-bar states. The offsets are in large-viewport units. The stage, if it is sized with a percentage of the initial containing block, is in small-viewport units. The difference is about the height of the browser chrome. Type that was centered with `vh` can be clipped by the hidden overflow, or can sit under the URL bar, depending on which box was used for the stage. Because `hidden` provides no scrollbar, the visitor cannot drag the word back into view. The scrub then appears broken rather than responsive: the word leaves the hole and does not come back until the numbers happen to fit again.
- `100vw` horizontal offsets are the usual way a one-screen type stage grows a sideways scrollbar. The CSS-Tricks account says the usual patch is `overflow: hidden` on a parent or on the body. That patch is what turns a one-pixel overflow into a clip of the words, and on iOS it also fights rubber-band scrolling. GSAP’s normalizeScroll removes that bounce on purpose; if the portrait’s only scroll is inside an `overflow: hidden` stage, there may be no native scroll left to scrub with.
- Absolute positioning against a stage means the scrub’s coordinate system is the stage, while `vw`/`vh` are the viewport. Mixing them (a stage in pixels or `svh`, a travel distance in `vw`) makes the same progress value land in different places after rotation, after the chrome moves, and after a scrollbar appears. The word can overlap itself, leave a gap, or clip on one side only. Dynamic viewport units (`dvh`) track the chrome but also change during the scroll that is driving the scrub, so a pin whose distance was computed in `dvh` changes length while the visitor is in it. That is the jump GSAP describes, moved into the unit itself.
- Fixed positioning would track the visible viewport, including URL-bar changes, per Chrome’s note, but a `transform` on an ancestor was not re-verified in this pass (see gaps). GSAP pins commonly transform the pinned element. If that transform re-binds a `fixed` descendant to the stage, the URL-bar exception Chrome describes would no longer apply. This note does not assert that rebinding as a fetched quotation.

### Gaps
- MDN’s page on CSS transforms was fetched, but the extractor did not return the sentence about `transform`, `filter`, or `perspective` creating a containing block for fixed and absolute descendants. That failure mode is not cited as fact here.
- No WebKit bug write-up was retrieved that says scroll events on iOS fire only when momentum ends. Do not use that claim.
- The CSS Values scrollbar clause (when `vw` does or does not subtract a classic scrollbar) was only partly captured. The CSS-Tricks description of the `100vw` horizontal overflow is the citation used. Do not add a browser-by-browser matrix.
- No device lab check was done. These are specification and vendor-doc failure modes, not screenshots of this portrait.

## Reduced motion: what must the static equivalent contain so the sentences are still the experience?

### Takeaway
Reduced motion removes the movement, not the information. If the sentences are the piece, the static page has to show those sentences, in order, readable without scrubbing and without waiting. A frozen animation frame that leaves lines at zero opacity or outside the clip is not a reduced-motion design; it deletes the content WCAG says must still be achievable another way.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 2.3.3’s intent is that people can prevent animation, because motion can distract or sicken, including when “scrolling a page causes elements to move (other than the essential movement associated with scrolling).” Vestibular reactions listed: dizziness, nausea, headaches. Allowed responses: do not add the non-essential animation, offer a control that turns it off, or honor the platform reduced-motion setting. Essential scrolling movement stays. — [Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- Essential means both that removal would fundamentally change the information or functionality and that there is no other conforming way to provide that information. Motion animation is defined there as steps between states that create the illusion of movement or a smooth transition. Opacity, blur, and color change are explicitly not “motion animation” for this criterion. Position changes of the words are. — [Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- Technique C39: honor `prefers-reduced-motion` and do not display the animation. It also documents the inverse pattern: write the static styles as the base, and put the motion in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)`. The test procedure is: turn the setting on, confirm the animation is not essential, confirm it no longer runs. — [C39: Using the CSS prefers-reduced-motion query to prevent motion](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/css/C39)
- Michelle Barker, Smashing Magazine: `prefers-reduced-motion` is `reduce` or `no-preference`. Putting animation only inside `no-preference` means older browsers that do not understand the query keep the motion-free element. She also turns off `scroll-behavior: smooth` under reduced motion, because a long smooth jump is unpleasant for motion sensitivity, and she notes the same media query can decide whether to run, or even load, a motion library. — [Respecting Users’ Motion Preferences](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2021/10/respecting-users-motion-preferences/)
- web.dev: the query is how a site offers a variant to people who asked the OS to minimize animation. Parallax, zooming, autoplaying video, and elements that move at a different rate from the scroll are the examples tied to vestibular disorders. The OS settings shown (macOS Reduce motion, Android Remove animations) are described as removing decorative motion such as app-launch effects, and sites are told to remove unnecessary animation too, not to remove the underlying content. `reduce` means minimize movement or animation, “preferably to the point where all non-essential movement is removed.” — [prefers-reduced-motion: Sometimes less movement is more](https://web.dev/articles/prefers-reduced-motion)
- CSS-Tricks’ WCAG animation walkthrough restates 2.2.2 and 2.3.3 and says longer automatic animations need a pause/play control the designer can style however they want. Its worked example of a responsible article animation: figures that would loop instead have play/stop, and one-shot decorative animations finish and then offer replay. The motion is opt-in; the text of the article is not behind the play button. — [Accessible Web Animation: The WCAG on Animation Explained](https://css-tricks.com/accessible-web-animation-the-wcag-on-animation-explained/)
- NN/g’s reading findings are the reason a portrait cannot substitute motion for the sentences: visitors scan, they read on the order of a fifth of the words, and they spend most of that attention at the top. — [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/); [How Little Do Users Read?](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/); [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)

### Inferences
- The static equivalent of a three- or four-line pinned sequence is those lines, set as normal text, in reading order, in the first screen, with no pin and no scroll distance required. That is what C39’s “static styles as the base” pattern produces, and it is what remains when `no-preference` is the only place the motion exists. It also satisfies the no-scroll end state from the pacing question, so one layout serves reduced motion, a broken scrub, and a visitor who never scrolls.
- What it must contain, because the sentences are the experience: every line the motion would have assembled, not a summary and not only the last line; the same wording, since the motion was not adding information a caption could replace; visual quiet that still reads as the piece (size, order, and the pause between lines) rather than a stack of captions under a blank stage. Grouping that makes the three or four lines one thought is the static version of the scrub. A single key line with the others dropped fails the “information cannot be achieved another way” test in reverse: the motion version had the information, the static version would not.
- What it must not be: the animation paused on a frame where lines are off-stage, clipped by `overflow: hidden`, or at opacity 0. WCAG’s exclusion of opacity from “motion animation” does not make an invisible line acceptable; the line is then simply absent. The CSS-Tricks pattern of “text always present, motion opt-in” is the shape that keeps the sentences as the experience.
- Reduced motion does not require a joyless page. web.dev and WCAG are about non-essential movement. A portrait can keep a still, severe typographic composition, which is the communication, and drop the travel. Smooth-scroll on any in-page jump should be off for this preference, per the Smashing article, because the jump itself is a large movement the person did not ask to see animated.
- Because this visitor arrived from X to a personal page, the static page also has to survive the 10-second value test on its own. The reduced-motion view is often the first view that is purely the writing. If that view is an empty stage, the page fails both the accessibility criterion and the dwell-time finding at once.

### Gaps
- No source was found that specifies a visual design for a “reduced-motion typography sequence” on a personal site (how large, how spaced, whether one line or all lines above the fold). The requirement that the sentences be present and readable is what the WCAG text supports. The layout details are not.
- A List Apart’s often-cited motion-sensitivity article returned 404 at the URL tried, so it is not quoted.
- These notes did not verify current browser support percentages for `prefers-reduced-motion`. Smashing Magazine’s claim of “excellent support in all modern browsers” as of that 2021 article was not re-measured.
